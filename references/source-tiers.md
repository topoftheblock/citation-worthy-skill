# Source maps by domain

Where the Tier 1 material actually lives, field by field. Read the section matching the
question you're researching, then go straight to those sources instead of searching the
open web and filtering afterwards.

## Science and engineering

**Tier 1** — the peer-reviewed article itself via DOI; the journal's own site; PMC for
open-access biomedical work; national labs and agencies (NASA, NOAA, NIST, CERN, ESA,
Max Planck, CNRS); standards bodies (ISO/IEC, IEEE, ASTM); systematic reviews and
meta-analyses (Cochrane); national academy reports; IPCC assessment reports.

**Tier 2** — arXiv/bioRxiv preprints (label as not peer reviewed); university press
releases about their own research (they overstate — always follow through to the paper);
society magazines and specialist science journalism with named science reporters.

**Skip** — "study finds" aggregators, university-ranking content farms, and any page
that reports an effect size without linking the paper.

**Watch for** — a press release describing a mouse study as a human treatment; a single
small study reported as consensus; a preprint that was never published.

## Medicine and public health

**Tier 1** — FDA/EMA labels and approval documents; CDC, WHO, NICE, NIH; clinical
practice guidelines from the relevant specialty society; Cochrane reviews;
ClinicalTrials.gov registrations; PubMed-indexed trials.

**Tier 2** — major medical journals' editorial content, academic medical centers,
professional-society patient material.

**Skip** — supplement vendors, wellness blogs, symptom-checker content farms, and
"natural cure" sites, regardless of how well they rank.

**Watch for** — guidance that has been superseded; country-specific approvals presented
as universal; dosage figures repeated across sites with no primary source.

## Law, policy and regulation

**Tier 1** — the statute or regulation as published (official gazettes, EUR-Lex,
govinfo.gov, Federal Register, national legislature sites); court opinions from the court
or CourtListener; agency rulemaking dockets; treaty texts from the depositary.

**Tier 2** — bar association analyses, law-school clinics, established legal press,
law-review articles.

**Skip** — law-firm SEO landing pages, "know your rights" content farms, partisan
advocacy summaries of a bill.

**Watch for** — jurisdiction (a US federal rule is not a state rule is not an EU
regulation); effective dates and amendments; bills that never passed being described as
law.

## Software, APIs and security

**Tier 1** — the project's own documentation, changelog, release notes, source, and issue
tracker; RFCs and W3C/WHATWG specs; the vendor's security advisory; NVD/CVE records;
CISA advisories; the language or runtime's own reference.

**Tier 2** — MDN, OWASP, maintainer blogs, conference talks by the maintainers,
specialist security press.

**Community, with care** — Stack Overflow, GitHub discussions, and technical blogs are
useful for debugging leads. Treat them as hypotheses to test in the code, and confirm
anything you'll state as fact against first-party docs. Always check the version the
answer targets.

**Skip** — tutorial mills, doc mirrors, scraped Q&A clones, "top 10 libraries" listicles,
and AI-written explainers with non-existent flags.

**Watch for** — version drift (the answer is right for v2, you're on v5); deprecated APIs;
platform differences.

## Economics, finance and companies

**Tier 1** — SEC EDGAR filings and their non-US equivalents; central banks (Fed, ECB,
BoE, BIS); IMF, World Bank, OECD; national statistical agencies (BLS, Eurostat, ONS);
company earnings releases and investor decks for facts about themselves.

**Tier 2** — Reuters, Bloomberg, AP, FT, WSJ news reporting; rating-agency methodology
documents.

**Skip** — stock-tip blogs, crypto promotion sites, affiliate broker comparisons,
"analyst" content farms.

**Watch for** — nominal vs real, seasonally adjusted vs not, revisions to prior figures,
fiscal vs calendar year, and forecasts reported as outcomes. Never present any of this as
investment advice.

## History and the humanities

**Tier 1** — digitized primary documents from national archives and libraries (Library of
Congress, National Archives, Bibliothèque nationale, Bundesarchiv); museum collection
records; scholarly editions; peer-reviewed history journals; university press monographs.

**Tier 2** — encyclopedias with signed articles (Britannica, Stanford Encyclopedia of
Philosophy), academic book reviews, historical societies.

**Skip** — nationalist and revisionist advocacy sites, "hidden history" content farms,
quote sites (a large share of famous quotations are misattributed — trace to a primary
text before using one).

**Watch for** — historiographical dispute presented as settled fact; translated sources
without a named translator; dates differing across calendars.

## Current events

**Tier 1** — wire services (AP, Reuters, AFP, PA) for what happened and when; official
statements, transcripts, and filings from the parties involved; live primary video or
documents.

**Tier 2** — established outlets with bylines and corrections policies; local outlets for
local events (often better sourced than national coverage).

**Skip** — partisan outlets on all sides, aggregators, social-media screenshots, and
"breaking" accounts with no editorial process.

**Watch for** — developing stories where early reports are wrong; a single source
laundered through many outlets until it looks corroborated; opinion pieces surfacing in
news searches.

## Statistics and datasets

**Tier 1** — the issuing statistical agency's own release and its methodology notes;
Eurostat, UN data portals, World Bank open data, OECD.Stat; the dataset's data dictionary.

**Watch for** — chart-only secondary sites that lose the definition of the measure; a
figure repeated across the web that traces back to one uncited blog post; base-year and
currency-conversion differences. Always report the measure's definition alongside the
number.

## Products, prices and consumer claims

**Tier 1** — the manufacturer's spec sheet and official pricing page; regulatory filings
(FCC, CE); standards test results.

**Tier 2** — independent testing organizations that buy what they test and take no
advertising; individual hands-on reviews in established trade press with a named
reviewer, a date, and a described test method. Most of that press earns affiliate revenue
on the products it reviews — say so in the answer, and prefer agreement between two
outlets over any single one.

**Skip** — affiliate review sites, "best of" listicles and roundup rankings (even on a
reputable outlet, use the underlying review rather than the ranking), coupon and deal
pages, and comparison tables published by a competitor. This is the single most content-farmed
category on the web; assume a fetchable page is monetized until shown otherwise.

**Watch for** — prices and availability change constantly; always report the date and the
region. "Best" is a judgment, not a fact: report what was measured and by whom, and let
the recommendation follow from that.
